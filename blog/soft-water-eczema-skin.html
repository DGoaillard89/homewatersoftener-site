Soft water and eczema: what we know, and what we don't

By HomeWaterSoftener · 8 February 2024

February is peak dry-skin season. The heating's been on for months, it's cold outside, and if anyone in your house has eczema, you'll know it often flares about now. So it's no surprise that this is when we get the most calls asking, "Will a water softener help my child's eczema?" It's a really fair question, and it deserves a straight answer rather than a sales pitch.

First, the important bit. We're water engineers, not doctors. A water softener isn't a medical treatment and we'd never tell you it will cure or treat eczema. If you're worried about anyone's skin, your GP, health visitor or pharmacist is the right first stop.

What hard water does on your skin

Hard water is full of dissolved calcium and magnesium. When soap meets those minerals, some of it reacts and forms a sticky scum instead of a lather. You've seen it as the grey ring round the bath. The same thing happens on your skin, and it's harder to rinse off than you'd think.

That's why lots of people say their skin feels tight or dry after a shower in hard water, and why the same shower gel seems to "go further" in soft water. You need less of it, it lathers easily and it rinses away cleanly. Some people find that more comfortable, especially in winter when skin's already having a tough time.

What the research says

This is where it's worth being careful, because there's a lot of overselling out there.

Researchers have looked at hard water and eczema for years. Some studies have found that young children living in hard-water areas seem more likely to have eczema than those in soft-water areas. A UK study published in 2016, which followed young babies, found a link between harder water at home and a higher chance of eczema developing in the first months of life. But a link isn't the same as cause and effect, and those studies don't show that softening your water would change anything.

The best test of that so far was a UK trial called the Softened Water Eczema Trial, published in 2011. Families of children with eczema had a softener fitted, and the children's skin was checked by nurses who didn't know which homes had one. The trial didn't find a meaningful difference in eczema severity from the softener. Interestingly, many parents in the trial did feel their child's skin had improved, but the independent assessments didn't back that up.

So the honest summary is: there's an interesting link between hard water and eczema, research is still going on, and there's no good evidence that a softener treats it. Anyone who tells you otherwise is getting ahead of the science.

So why do people still fit one?

Because comfort matters, even if it isn't medicine. Plenty of families tell us that bath time just feels nicer with softened water: less soap needed, less residue, towels that aren't scratchy. Some find it easier to rinse moisturisers and washes off properly. None of that is a health claim. It's just how soft water feels, and lots of people like it.

And the other benefits are real and measurable: far less limescale on taps, showers and glass, less scale building up in your boiler and appliances, and a lot less scrubbing. If you were thinking about a softener for those reasons anyway, the nicer feel at bath time is a bonus.

Practical tips for winter skin in a hard-water home

Whether or not you ever get a softener, these are the kinds of everyday things that skin specialists commonly suggest. Check with your GP or pharmacist what's right for your family, especially for babies and young children.

  • Keep baths and showers warm, not hot, and fairly short.
  • Use a gentle, soap-free wash rather than bubble bath or strongly scented products.
  • Rinse well, so nothing's left sitting on the skin.
  • Pat dry with a soft towel rather than rubbing.
  • Moisturise straight after, using whatever your GP or pharmacist has recommended.
  • Watch the laundry. Hard water needs more detergent, and detergent residue in clothes and bedding can bother sensitive skin. An extra rinse cycle can help.

A word on drinking water and babies

If you do fit a softener, one thing to know: softened water isn't recommended for making up baby formula, because softening adds sodium. That's why we fit a separate unsoftened drinking tap in the kitchen with every softener we install. You get softened water for bathing and washing, and normal mains water for drinking, cooking and feeds. Quick answers

Does a water softener cure eczema?

No. A water softener isn't a medical treatment, and the main UK trial didn't find that softeners reduced eczema severity. Some families simply find softened water more comfortable for bathing. Speak to your GP about any skin condition.

Why does my skin feel different in soft water?

Soap reacts with the minerals in hard water and forms a residue that's hard to rinse off. In soft water, soap lathers easily and rinses away cleanly, so skin often feels less tight afterwards.

Can I use softened water for baby formula?

It isn't recommended, because softening adds sodium. We fit a separate unsoftened drinking tap with every softener, so you've always got normal mains water for drinking and feeds.
