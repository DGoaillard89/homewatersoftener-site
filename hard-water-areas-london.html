    Short version: if you're in London, your water's hard. Almost everywhere in the capital and the South East gets hard or very hard water, typically 250–300+ mg/l as calcium carbonate. Some streets come in a bit lower, some higher, but you'd struggle to find properly soft water anywhere inside the M25. Here's why, what the numbers mean, and how to check yours.

    Why London's water is so hard

    It's down to geology. A lot of London's water comes from the River Thames and the River Lee, and from groundwater pumped up from underground. On its way there, much of that water has soaked through chalk and limestone. Rain is naturally a little acidic, so as it trickles through the rock it dissolves calcium and magnesium and carries them along. That's what makes water "hard".

    None of that's a health problem. Hard water's perfectly safe to drink. It's the limescale that's the nuisance: in kettles, on taps, on shower screens and inside boilers and hot water cylinders. Our post on hard water and your boiler explains what's going on behind the scenes.

    What the hardness numbers mean

    Hardness is usually given in mg/l (milligrams per litre) as calcium carbonate. These are the bands we use on our site:

    • Soft: 0–50 mg/l
    • Moderately soft: 51–100 mg/l
    • Slightly hard: 101–150 mg/l
    • Moderately hard: 151–200 mg/l
    • Hard: 201–300 mg/l
    • Very hard: over 300 mg/l

    You'll sometimes see other units, especially on softener settings and test kits:

    • Clark degrees (°Clark): divide mg/l by 14.3. So 300 mg/l is about 21 °Clark.
    • German degrees (°dH): divide mg/l by 17.8. So 300 mg/l is about 17 °dH.

    Most of London sits in the hard or very hard bands.

    The Home Counties

    It's much the same story outside London. We also cover Surrey, Kent, Essex, Hertfordshire, Buckinghamshire and Berkshire, where a lot of the water also comes from chalk and limestone and tends to be hard or very hard.     How to check your water hardness

    There are a few easy ways:

    • Use our checker. Pop your postcode into the checker at the top of this page. The figures are an approximate guide to what's typical for your area, not official supplier data, but they're plenty to tell you whether a softener's worth thinking about.
    • Check your supplier's website. Thames Water, Affinity Water, SES Water, South East Water and Essex & Suffolk Water all publish hardness by postcode.
    • Get it tested. We test your water for free as part of every installation survey, so we can set any softener up for your actual supply.

    There's more detail on all of this in our post: is my area hard water?

    What you can do about it

    You've got two real options. You can live with it: descale the kettle, wipe down the taps and keep an eye on the boiler. Or you can fit a whole-house water softener, which takes the hardness out where the mains comes in, so limescale stops building up all through the house. We always fit a separate unsoftened drinking tap in the kitchen alongside it. Find out how it works on our water softener installation page.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is London water hard everywhere?

    Pretty much. Nearly all of London gets hard or very hard water, typically 250–300+ mg/l as calcium carbonate. Levels vary a little from area to area, but soft water is rare inside the M25.

    Is hard water safe to drink?

    Yes. Hard water is safe to drink. The calcium and magnesium in it cause limescale, but they're not a health risk.

    How accurate is the postcode checker?

    It's an approximate guide to what's typical for each postcode area, not official supplier data. Your exact figure can vary street by street and through the year. For a precise reading, check your water supplier's website or ask us to test it during a free survey.

    Who supplies my water, and where can I get official figures?

    Your water bill names your supplier. Across London and the Home Counties it's most often Thames Water, Affinity Water or SES Water, though other companies cover parts of Surrey, Kent and Essex. Each one lets you look up the hardness for your postcode.

    What's the difference between mg/l and Clark degrees?

    They're just different units. To convert mg/l as calcium carbonate to Clark degrees, divide by 14.3. For German degrees (°dH), divide by 17.8.

    Does hard water damage boilers?

    Limescale can build up inside boilers, hot water cylinders and heating elements over time, which makes them work harder. Softening the water stops new scale forming.
